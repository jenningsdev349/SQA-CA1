# Guide To Task Estimation in Scrum
### Contributors: Adam Jennings & Hamzah Naveid

## Introduction

Task estimations are a crucial part of scrum and are vital to healthy team workflows, alongside helping to bring consistent deliverables as work is completed. This article will discuss the importance of task estimation, why it can be difficult, and ways to overcome those difficulties.

## 1. Why Are Task Estimations important?
Task estimations are of huge importance for team productivity. Task estimation serves to benefit both developers and non-developers within the team. 

For non-developers in the team, estimates help to:

- #### Plan and coordinate product releases
- #### Ensure that the resources in the organisation go to good use

For developers, key benefits include being able to:
- #### Synchronise work easier with other teams 
- #### Deliver quality work in the least possible amount of time
- #### Manage scope creep and prevent it from occurring
- #### Structure and plan out work in a logical and efficient way

![Task Estimation Importance](/images/importance-of-software-development-time.png)

Accurately estimating tasks can be another issue entirely. The pressure to provide accurate task estimations and stick to them can be difficult for developers to grapple with. Providing numbers as to how long a task will take is easy, but if that number is unfounded and inaccurate, it can very often do more damage due to dependencies that other teams may have on a particular task to be completed than if there was no estimate provided at all. Given this issue, it is crucial that teams follow tried and true rules and methods to deliver accurate estimates that reflect the actual length of time a task will take to see to fruition. 

## 2. Why Is Estimating Task Length Difficult?

Before we understand the methods a team can use to accurately estimate task length, we must first discuss what makes task estimation such a difficult process to begin with. 

### Software Complexity 
Codebases are very often poorly understood by most developers in a team. Members in a team may have expert insights into specific areas of the codebase, but anything beyond the code the developer personally works on can be hard to understand for them. Even for the areas of a codebase a team member works on, issues may regularly arise with creating changes due to the complexities of the software. Because of this, a lot of the time a developer spends working is spent on research and looking at prior completed work. When combining all of these factors together, we can assert that even for small changes to a codebase - we cannot be sure with complete and total accuracy how long a task will take to finish. 

### Task Ambiguity
Ambiguity in task requirements can be a significant deterrent to accurately estimating task length. In the early stages of a project, the people in a team who are putting forth task estimations can lack technical knowledge which increases difficulty of task estimation. As part of scrum, requirements are worked out as a project progresses. Things change, the stakeholders in the project may want a new feature or a change in the features already present in the software. Other times, tasks may not have clear requirements and thus assumptions are made on what should be done. 

### Scope Creep
![Scope Creep](/images/scope-creep.png)

Teams can often start with a software estimate (i.e. a task should be completed within 2 weeks), and only then will the team discuss the work that should be done to fit that 2 week schedule. If a developer is given 2 weeks to work on a task, they will be driven to find the quickest solution that actually works that can be implemented into the codebase ASAP. Give that same developer 6 months to work on the same task, and the work completed and features added as part of that task will skyrocket as the developer has been given space to apply additional scope to the task. This means that the work completed as part of tasks is variable, and since multiple valid solutions exist for any piece of development work - the team is often not estimating a stable target.

## 3. Effective and Accurate Task Estimation

There are numerous approaches to take that can counteract the challenges and difficulties associated with task estimation.

### Breaking Tasks Down
Break larger tasks into subtasks that can be addressed separately - either independently by individuals, or teams working in parallel - and then joined together later on. By breaking tasks into smaller, more manageable units, a better assessment of the effort and time required for each component of the project is gained.

### Using Past Experiences
For more complex tasks, looking at past work done and using that as a comparison can provide an idea of how long it typically takes to complete similar tasks.

### Employing Estimation Techniques
![Planning Poker](/images/planning-poker-correct-steps.png)

Utilising techniques such as Planning Poker greatly increases estimate accuracy and encourage collaboration and communication. The estimates that are produced by these techniques tend to be more realistic as they better incorporate the complexities involved in the evolution of scrum deliveries over time.

### Factor In Uncertainty
Instead of providing a concrete, single estimate - offer a range that reflects the degree of uncertainty involved in the task. Allow estimations to have a buffer for the "human factor", adding 20 - 25% on top to account for people getting sick, third-party vendors being slow to respond, bureaucracy, and misunderstandings. This will help set realistic expectations and allow flexibility as the project evolves.

## Conclusion
Task estimation has a significant role to play in the success of a project before any code is written. This process has several knock-on effects on the health of the project down the line, so it is crucial to get it right. Identifying the pain points of task estimation provides a path towards putting counteracting measures in place that allow for more effective and accurate task estimation. The result is having a better understanding of the requirements and reasonable expectations before starting a project - laying the groundwork for a successful project.
